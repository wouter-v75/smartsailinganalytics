# Rate of turn, turn radius and steering geometry — Northstar 76, 2026 season

*26 September 2026. Every number is measured from the boat's own logs. Where the
logs cannot support a measurement it is left out rather than estimated, and the
coverage is stated beside each figure.*

---

## 1. The three numbers, and why they differ

**Rate of turn** — heading change over the **6 seconds centred on the moment the
apparent wind crosses the bow**, divided by the span actually measured. The
average through the fastest part of the turn.

**Turn radius** — **speed ÷ yaw rate** over that same window. The radius the boat
actually turned at. This is the operating point any steering geometry has to be
correct for, and unlike the geometry it can be measured without knowing anything
about the boat.

**Max rotation** — the single fastest heading change between two consecutive
samples. A *peak*, which rises with the log's own sampling noise.

Compared **on the same manoeuvres**, max rotation reads higher in **94 of 98
tacks** and **44 of 44 gybes**, by a median of **1.00 °/s** and **1.19 °/s**. That
gap is the sampling, not the sailing. A target set from max rotation would be a
target nobody is actually sailing.

Timing the window on the apparent wind rather than on the manoeuvre detector
matters: on 12 September the crossing lands a consistent **+2.7 s after** the
detected moment, so timing from the detector would have measured a different part
of every turn.

### What the season can carry

| | tacks | gybes |
|---|---|---|
| Rate of turn (±3 s window) | **98 of 587**, on 8 of 23 days | **44 of 230**, on 6 of 22 days |
| Turn radius | **81** | **42** |
| Max rotation | **546** | **220** |

Rate of turn needs a log sampled at **3 s or finer**; half the season is logged at
6.09 s, and a six-second window sampled every six seconds is not a measurement.
Turn radius needs the rate *and* a real turn under it — **R = v/ω runs away as ω
goes to zero**, so it is only computed above 2 °/s. Max rotation survives a coarse
log, which is why it covers five times as many manoeuvres, and why it should be
read as a ceiling rather than a performance.

**It is not a bell.** A hump at 5–7 °/s and a tail of slow ones running to nearly
zero. The mean of **5.25 °/s** sits below the median of **5.73** because that tail
drags it there; the typical tack is the median.

The bottom bar is not slow tacking. **Fifteen of the ninety-eight** sit near zero,
and a tack that turns at 0.3 °/s is something recorded as a tack that was not one,
or a stretch where the heading data is bad. They are excluded from the radius for
that reason.

## 4. Summary

- The typical tack turns at **5.7 °/s** in **43 m**; the typical gybe at
  **4.8 °/s** in **50 m**. Medians, from the ±3 s window.
- The boat turns in **roughly two boat lengths**, and tacks are the tighter of
  the two manoeuvres.
- The tack rate distribution is **not normal** — a hump with a tail of slow ones.
  The tail is where the time goes, not the average.
- **Rate of turn is independent of wind speed** (R² 0.01). If it is to be
  improved, the variable is not the breeze.
- **Fifteen of the ninety-eight rated tacks look like bad records rather than bad
  tacks** and should be checked before any mean is quoted onward.
- Max rotation reads higher on the same manoeuvre almost every time. Use it for
  coverage, not as a target.

## 5. Steering geometry

### The operating point, measured

This is what turn radius buys: the geometry only has to be right where the boat
actually sails, and that is now known rather than assumed.

Treating the boat as a bicycle — a single effective steering angle at an effective
distance **L** ahead of the turn centre — the helm carried through each turn gives

> **L = R · tan δ**

Across **68 tacks** that lands at a median **12.6 m**
(range 5.5–19.6), and across
**36 gybes** at **11.7 m**. The two agree, which
they need not have, and both are close to **half the hull length**.

That is an *effective* pivot distance, not a tape measurement: a hull generates
side force along its whole length, so it will not equal the geometric distance
from the rudder stock to the centre of lateral resistance. It is, however, the
number the boat behaves as though it has — **worth comparing against the tape
measurement rather than replaced by it.**

At the measured operating point — radius **43 m**, L **12.6 m** — the helm angle
implied is **16.3°**, which matches the top of the measured steering range.

### The linkage, measured

Across **78,149 log rows** carrying both rudder angles, fitting starboard
against port gives

> **ruddS = 1.0008 × ruddP − 1.215**   (R² 0.932)

A slope of **1.0008 is parallel steering**: within these sensors' resolution the
two rudders turn by the same amount, with a **constant offset of about −1.2°**
rather than a progressive differential.

Under true Ackermann the inner rudder turns **more** than the outer, making
starboard − port **positive** in both directions. It is negative and grows more
negative. **The boat is running slightly anti-Ackermann.**

### The target, once the rudder separation is known

With **L = 12.6 m measured** and the operating radius of **43 m**, the only
missing number is **t**, the separation between the rudder stocks. Inner and outer
angles at that operating point:

| t (m) | t / L | inner | outer | differential needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.0 | 0.317 | 17.08° | 15.64° | **+1.44°** |
| 4.5 | 0.357 | 17.18° | 15.56° | **+1.62°** |
| 5.0 | 0.397 | 17.28° | 15.48° | **+1.80°** |
| 5.5 | 0.437 | 17.38° | 15.40° | **+1.98°** |
| 6.0 | 0.476 | 17.48° | 15.32° | **+2.17°** |

Against a **measured −2.91°** at that helm. Whatever t turns out to be, the
correction is of the order of **4.4° to 5.1° at full helm** — reversing the sign
and adding the Ackermann. That is large enough to be worth getting right, and
large enough that guessing t would have been irresponsible.

### The one thing that must happen first

**These are sensor readings, not linkage geometry.** A constant −1.2° offset is
indistinguishable, from the log alone, from one rudder's angle sensor being zeroed
1.2° differently from the other. **Zero both sensors dockside against physically
aligned rudders and record the offsets before anything mechanical is touched.** If
the offset proves to be calibration, what remains is the ~0.1 °/° drift, which is
small — and the whole picture changes.

### In order

1. **Zero both rudder sensors** against aligned rudders, dockside. Until this is
   done the −1.2° cannot be interpreted at all.
2. **Measure t**, the rudder stock separation, and put it on the boat record
   alongside the geometric rudder-to-CLR distance — then compare that against the
   **12.6 m** the boat behaves as though it has.
3. **Get more days onto a 1–2 s log.** Rate of turn and radius exist on 8 days of
   23 only because the rest are logged at 6.09 s. That one change would quadruple
   the sample under every number in sections 2 and 3.
4. **Then** the table above gives the target at the radius the boat actually
   sails, the measured curve gives the error, and a linkage change can be
   specified rather than guessed.
