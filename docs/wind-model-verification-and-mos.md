# Seven masts beat 240 garden anemometers

The decisive finding is a sorting problem, not a statistics problem: of SSA's ~240 wind stations, only the **professional subset — 7 Port-Log harbour masts, the Météo-France RADOME stations, MF and NDBC buoys, METAR and the 2 Channel Coastal Observatory piers, roughly fifteen sites** — is fit to carry a MOS fit, and the 225 Davis WeatherLink garden stations are display-only. That is not a conservative preference; it is the explicit verdict of the only two national centres that have evaluated crowd-sourced wind. The Met Office concluded that "**for wind information the observation rejection rate is too high to be useful**" and that its citizen anemometers "showed **no cohesion in their data**" ([Met Office EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_MetOffice_Final_2022.pdf)), while KNMI's parallel study put a number on it: of **37,422** private stations reporting wind, about **7,000 survived within-station QC — roughly 19 %** ([KNMI EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_KNMI_Final_2022.pdf)). Three further conclusions follow and each changes a design decision already made in the codebase. **Spatial (2D) verification is better in principle and unavailable in practice** — four of the five spatial method families need a gridded truth field, and the only genuine 2-D wind verification in the literature manufactured its truth from a 155-station network driving a diagnostic model ([Schlager et al. 2019](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/12/2855/2019/)); the single-observation neighbourhood route (HiRA/SO-NF) plus explicit feature-timing verification is what scattered points actually support. **The height correction should run downwards, not upwards**: transferring the model to the station's own level keeps the model's own stability and roughness internally consistent and reduces an unknown anemometer height to one fitted per-sector coefficient, where lifting the observation to 10 m forces a guess the station cannot support — and the dominant error is not roughness but stability, which in an ordinary stable coastal morning makes the true 10→30 m increase **+20 to +30 % instead of +9 %**. And **the MOS that already ships was fitted against three years of one boat's masthead anemometer**, which means its intercepts (+0.8 to +6.3 kn across the three venues) contain that instrument's calibration, masthead upwash and a missing stability term as well as model bias; an observation-based refit measures a different quantity and will not reproduce them. The one action with no substitute and a hard deadline is retention: observations are pruned at **90 days**, local MOS fits in the literature want **365–1816 days** ([Lerch & Baran](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1509.03521)), and every day not kept is gone, because the upstream networks hold 24 hours to 45 days.

## Only about fifteen of 240 stations can carry a fit, and the test that finds the bad ones is not a QC test

The usability ordering across crowd-sourced variables is consistent across both centre studies — pressure ≈ temperature, then rainfall, then wind a long way last — and the reason is physical rather than statistical. A cheap thermometer in a garden measures garden air, which a distribution-plus-buddy check can partly correct; a cheap anemometer at 2 m in a garden measures garden turbulence, and the signal is gone. The Met Office states the mechanism directly: WMO wants a 10 m mast with few obstructions, "in a small well-developed environment such as a private garden, it is unlike[ly] that most PWSs will be able to achieve this and therefore **major errors will be introduced. The turbulence at most sites will be severe. These large and variable errors will not be easy to correct**" ([Met Office EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_MetOffice_Final_2022.pdf)). The corroborating structural evidence is that **CrowdQC and CrowdQC+ have no wind module after two package generations** — they cover air temperature only and propose pressure and humidity, not wind, as extensions ([Fenner et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2021.720747/full)) — because the wisdom-of-the-crowd premise fails for 10 m wind: neighbouring garden anemometers disagree for real reasons. Note carefully what KNMI's 19 % measures: survival of *within-station* QC only (isolation, range, step, persistence), before any comparison with a reference. The fraction that is also representative enough to verify a model against is necessarily smaller and is reported nowhere.

The quantified siting penalty is what makes this irreducible. The WMO/ISO siting classification (ISO 19289:2014, WMO-No. 8 Annex 1.B) assigns a class **per variable, not per station**, and for wind it attaches an additional siting uncertainty of **up to 30 % at class 2 with correction possible, up to 50 % at class 3 with correction explicitly not applicable, over 50 % at class 4, and undefined at class 5** ([Leroy 2010](https://www.jma.go.jp/jma/en/Activities/qmws_2010/Presentation/S202_Leroy.pdf)). That is the harshest siting penalty of any variable in the scheme, and the class-2/class-3 boundary is the only line that matters for SSA: class 2 says a bias correction is legitimate, class 3 says it is fitting noise. The scheme also **forces class 4 or 5, with flag S, for any measurement below 10 m** — which places every station of unknown height epistemically on the uncorrectable side of that line, since you cannot assert a ≤30 % siting error without knowing the height. Chinese field experiments with AWS arrays at 30–300 m from obstacles found **more than 30 % speed attenuation at 10 times the obstacle height and over 80 % at 5 times** ([Guo Jianxia et al., TECO-2014](https://old.wmo.int/extranet/pages/prog/www/IMOP/publications/IOM-116_TECO-2014/Session%202/O2_WD_Jianxia_Experiment_simulations_siting_classification_for_wind_and_temperature.pdf)), which means the familiar "ten times obstacle height" rule is not a clean-exposure criterion at all but roughly the class-2 boundary. Crucially the sheltering error is **direction-dependent**: Météo-France diagnosed a class-4 site at St-Sulpice by the ratio of 10-minute mean speeds between two nearby masts split by direction sector, south winds against north ([Leroy 2010](https://www.jma.go.jp/jma/en/Activities/qmws_2010/Presentation/S202_Leroy.pdf)). A single multiplicative station factor cannot capture that; a per-sector one can.

The hardest part is that **siting faults are caught by nothing in a station-series QC battery**. HadISD's published wind battery — logical wind checks, repeated streaks, odd clusters, unusual variance, and an annual wind-rose consistency test — contains **no spike check and no nearest-neighbour check for wind at all**, only for temperature, dewpoint and pressure ([Dunn et al. 2016](https://gi.copernicus.org/articles/5/473/2016/gi-5-473-2016.xml)). That omission is deliberate: a spike threshold tight enough to catch a sensor glitch rejects real gusts, and a buddy check assumes a spatial coherence 10 m wind does not have at station spacing. A sheltered anemometer produces internally consistent, physically legal, plausible data forever. It is visible only against a reference: an exposure survey, a per-sector speed ratio against a nearby mast, or O−B bias monitoring — which is exactly what ECMWF's **monthly monitoring blocklist** is built from, "discarding the stations that have recently been reporting in an excessively noisy or biased manner compared with the ECMWF background field" ([IFS CY50R1 Part I](https://www.ecmwf.int/sites/default/files/elibrary/062026/81731-ifs-documentation-cy50r1-part-1-observations.pdf)). And the Met Office adds the warning that kills the cheap version: QC "can remove large errors, but we may find it hard to identify small offsets, **especially if they are common to a network**" — a network-wide sheltering bias among 225 Davis stations is structurally invisible to a buddy check among them.

### The witness channel generalises, but the witness is the model

SSA's frozen-feed detector works because heel is sensed independently off the IMU and cannot freeze the same way the masthead feed does; a channel bit-identical for longer than a helmsman holds it, *while heel proves the boat was moving*, is a dead feed. Asking what the analogue is for a fixed station is the right question, and the honest answer ranks four candidates by how independent they really are. The gust channel from the same sensor is the weakest: a seized bearing freezes mean and gust together, so gust is a witness only against a logger fault, not an anemometer fault — though a **gust below the mean is an internal-consistency violation with no published threshold anywhere in the located literature**, and is worth implementing anyway. A different variable from the same station (pressure tendency, temperature) proves the datalogger is alive and says nothing about whether the cups are turning. A neighbouring station is a buddy check, which HadISD specifically declines to apply to wind and which the Met Office warns against for correlated faults. That leaves the **NWP background as the only genuinely independent witness**, and ECMWF already runs precisely this rule: "AIREP wind observations with **zero wind speed** are rejected if the background wind exceeds **5 m s⁻¹**" ([IFS CY50R1 Part I](https://www.ecmwf.int/sites/default/files/elibrary/062026/81731-ifs-documentation-cy50r1-part-1-observations.pdf)). This report's judgement is that the generalisation holds with one uncomfortable consequence: the witness for a fixed wind station is the model you are trying to verify, which inverts the dependency. That is acceptable for gross faults (stuck at zero, stuck at a value while the model says 20 kn) and inadmissible for bias, because a station rejected for disagreeing with the model can no longer tell you the model is wrong. The frozen-versus-genuine-calm collision has exactly one documented solution and SSA should copy it verbatim: **branch the persistence test into a zero and a non-zero arm**, as the KNMI/Chen battery does, because genuine calm and a dead sensor are identical in a single persistence test ([KNMI EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_KNMI_Final_2022.pdf)). The coordinator's note that no published consecutive-value threshold exists for a frozen wind sensor is confirmed: HadISD computes its streak thresholds **dynamically per station** and never prints the resulting values, and the Chen/KNMI numbers live in a paywalled QJRMS paper and the [QCwind repository](https://github.com/jieyu97/QCwind). SSA will have to calibrate its own and should do so against the trusted masts, where a long clean record makes the distribution of legitimate streak lengths measurable.

The recommended test order is cheap first, diagnostic last: range and gross error (the KNMI data contained stations reporting **over 1,500 km/h** and some reporting wind speed **as a string**), then logical consistency on the direction–speed pairing (speed < 0 invalid; direction outside 0–360 invalid; direction 0° with non-zero speed invalid; calm with non-zero direction invalid, per [Dunn et al. 2016](https://gi.copernicus.org/articles/5/473/2016/gi-5-473-2016.xml)), then persistence split by zero and non-zero, then variance collapse, then streaks, then wind-rose stability, then O−B bias and noise monitoring, then exposure classification. Two cautions on what this will and will not do. The wind-rose test finds the *year a vane orientation changed*, not a constant offset present from installation, and in HadISD it is by far the most aggressive wind test by volume — **1,038 stations lost more than 2 % of their observations to it and 439 lost more than 5 %**. And a unit-conversion error of exactly 1.94 (kn↔m/s) or 3.6 (m/s↔km/h) sits inside the physically legal range and will pass every test in the list; no published test for it was found. For blacklist maintenance the only concrete cadence in any of the sources is the Met Office recommendation of "**3 rejects in a month** and a system manager puts it back", alongside a manual blacklist and an optional **superstation list of sites shown to be consistently good** ([Met Office EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_MetOffice_Final_2022.pdf)). That superstation list is the right shape for SSA's fit tier.

One station deserves naming. Cap Camarat is a wind-only RADOME station **107 m up on a headland, 4 nm from the race area**. It has the two properties that make a station unusable for MOS even when its instrument is perfect: its height is far outside any surface-layer extrapolation that reaches a 30 m masthead, and a headland is valid only for the direction sectors with water fetch, where the land sectors carry a completely different roughness (0.03–1.0 m against 2×10⁻⁴) and from the derived table below an **8–34 % different masthead correction** ([Leroy's class-4 headland case](https://www.jma.go.jp/jma/en/Activities/qmws_2010/Presentation/S202_Leroy.pdf)). Cap Camarat is a first-rate *gradient-wind and sector* indicator and a bad MOS target. Keeping it in the display and out of the fit is the correct asymmetry, and the same logic puts the Lymington Starting Platform, Hurst Castle and Yarmouth Pier WeatherFile stations — pier sites with tens to a few hundred metres of water fetch — into a probation tier rather than straight into the fit when their API token arrives.

## Spatial verification is the right answer to a question SSA's network cannot ask

The community taxonomy from the ICP splits spatial methods into **filtering** (neighbourhood, scale separation) and **displacement** (feature/object-based, field deformation) ([Gilleland et al. 2009](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/intercomparison-of-spatial-forecast-verification-methods/)), and the single fact that settles the question for SSA is that **four of those five families require two gridded fields**. Object methods need objects identified in the observations too, and they need a thresholding step that wind resists: wind vectors have "nonzero variability everywhere and no guaranteed well-defined objects" ([Buschow & Friederichs 2021](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/14/6765/2021/gmd-14-6765-2021.html)). Field deformation needs a continuous target field to morph onto. Wavelet methods need gap-free, periodic-ready images. Only the neighbourhood family has a point-observation variant.

| Family | Needs a gridded truth? | Usable from SSA's network |
|---|---|---|
| Neighbourhood, both-fields (FSS, WFSS, upscaling) | Yes | No — requires an observed fraction field |
| Neighbourhood, single-observation (SO-NF / HiRA) | No | **Yes** |
| Object-based (MODE, SAL, DIST, CRA) | Yes | No |
| Field deformation (Keil & Craig DAS, optical flow) | Yes | No |
| Scale separation / wavelet (Casati, SAD) | Yes | No |

The negative result is worth stating as strongly as the sources support it: Buschow and Friederichs name the obstacles as (i) a lack of spatial wind observations, (ii) methods built for scalar fields decomposable by thresholding, and (iii) precipitation's head start from radar, and they note that model analyses are sometimes used as stand-ins which "would need separate validation first". Every genuine 2-D wind verification located obtained its truth in one of four ways, none of them a plain station network: a diagnostic wind model driven by a very dense network, a mesoscale analysis, a radar-derived scalar proxy, or a spectral comparison against a reanalysis. The strongest case in the literature, the wind Fractions Skill Score, is instructive about the cost: Schlager et al. verified against a **100 m × 100 m, 30-minute field from the WegenerNet Wind Product Generator — a diagnostic model driving CALMET with 155 stations in one small region**, binning direction into eight 45° classes and speed into three, classing anything below 0.5 m s⁻¹ as calm, and recomputing the score over **nine rotations of the class boundaries in 5° steps and keeping the maximum** ([Schlager et al. 2019](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/12/2855/2019/)). SSA's Solent density of ~40 stations in 20 nm looks superficially comparable to Feldbach's 155, but 225 of 240 stations network-wide are garden anemometers of unknown height and class 3–5 exposure; driving a diagnostic wind model from those would produce a truth field whose small-scale structure is sheltering, not wind.

### What to do instead, and what it buys

The legitimate neighbourhood route is **HiRA / SO-NF**: keep the forecast neighbourhood around each trusted station, treat the surrounding grid values as an ensemble on the premise that "every grid square forecast is equi-probable at the observing site", and score that as a probability forecast with a proper rule ([Mittermaier 2014 via EMS2015](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EMS2015/orals/19535); [MET Point-Stat](https://metplus.readthedocs.io/projects/met/en/latest/Users_Guide/point-stat.html)). This credits the model for having the right value nearby without letting the observation choose. The temptation to implement next door — MET's *Best* interpolation, "the grid point inside the interpolation area whose value most closely matches the observation value" — must be refused, and MET's own documentation names the mechanism: "**unlike the other methods, it selects a grid value based on the observation**". The consequence, derived rather than cited, is that the reported error becomes the distribution of the *minimum* of k errors instead of a single error: bias is pulled toward zero regardless of whether the model is biased, RMSE falls monotonically as the neighbourhood grows, a model with **more** small-scale noise scores **better**, and the result is unusable for MOS because the forecast it reports was never issuable in advance. Note also Mittermaier's own stated weakness of SO-NF, which bites hardest at exactly SSA's venues: the method is "ignorant" of whether grid squares in the neighbourhood are land or sea. MET's *Geography Match* — nearest grid point satisfying land/sea and topography criteria — exists for this, and for a coastal mast it is the right interpolation. Plain bilinear interpolation across a coastline mixes land and sea grid points and should not be used; Patra et al. chose **nearest-neighbour selection specifically to avoid mixing land and ocean values at the coast** ([Patra et al. 2025](https://asr.copernicus.org/articles/22/69/2025/)).

The second substitute is better than a score: **report the displacement as a number with units**. For a sea breeze an hour late or 2 km inshore, the sea-breeze literature detects front passage at each site in both the observed and forecast time series and reports timing error in minutes — RAMS verification in Florida matched forecast and observed passage at individual wind towers and "for all 'hits', calculate[d] timing errors" ([NASA AMU](https://kscweather.ksc.nasa.gov/amu/files/briefings/fy01/18WAF-RAMSVerif.pdf)). This needs only point observations, and for a propagating front converting space to time is legitimate: 2 km inshore at a 5 kn front speed is about 26 minutes (this report's arithmetic). "Front arrived 55 min late, post-frontal speed within 0.4 kn" is a useful statement to a tactician; an RMSE at fixed times is not, and by the double-penalty arithmetic will actively prefer a model that produced no front at all. That arithmetic is worth spelling out because it governs the next question. Take ten grid boxes with an observed 12 kn maximum in boxes 5–6 and 6 kn elsewhere. A sharp forecast with the same amplitude, shape and width but two boxes late scores **MSE 14.4 kn², RMSE 3.79 kn**; a smooth forecast of 7.2 kn everywhere scores **MSE 5.76 kn², RMSE 2.40 kn** — the forecast that got everything right except position is **2.5× worse in MSE than one that predicted no feature**, and both score a critical success index of 0 at a 10 kn threshold (this report's arithmetic, following the framing in [Schlager et al. 2019](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/12/2855/2019/)). One caution on the detector: the Contour Error Map technique's reported failure mode was that "nearly all failures … resulted from complex precipitation features that contaminated the wind field", so an automatic front detector needs a precipitation filter — a squall outflow looks like a sea-breeze front in a wind time series.

### Does the 1–2 km nest help or hurt?

Both, and in a way that determines how it should be verified. The cautionary case is MET Norway's move from 10 km to 1 km, which "got larger errors along the coast" because the surface scheme changed from proportional land/sea fractions to tiles that were either ocean or land; the fix was a hand-built weighting emphasising the land component plus additional post-processing before public presentation ([2020 IVMW-O session notes](https://webdata.img.univie.ac.at/jwgvfr/Representativeness/Notes_2020IVMWO_Representiveness&ObsUncertainty_10Nov15UTC.pdf)). The land–sea transition in a model spans "only tens of kilometres" and the model's 10 m weak bias grows from about **6 % over open ocean to 12 % near the coast** ([Ingleby, Met Office poster](https://cscamm.umd.edu/programs/das6/program/Posters/uploads/Ip10-Ingleby_Bruce.pdf) — read via search extraction of an image-based PDF, so treat the two percentages as needing confirmation). This report's judgement is that the nest is worth keeping and must be verified differently from the global models: verify it against **sea stations only** — Bramble Bank, Nab Tower, the MF and NDBC buoys — never against a land or garden station whose grid cell has just become a pure land tile, and expect its advantage to appear in **feature timing and structure, not in RMSE at fixed times**, because by the arithmetic above a sharper model is penalised precisely when it is right about the feature and slightly wrong about its position. A 1–2 km nest is the only configuration SSA runs that can represent a sea-breeze front's geometry at all; a 9 km ICON cannot, and a 31 km reanalysis certainly cannot. Judging it on RMSE will retire it.

Finally, on the gridded references: **CERRA at 5.5 km is a defensible climatological reference and not an hour-by-hour truth field**. It is the literature's preferred coastal reference over Europe, with median NRMSE under 6 % and correlation above 0.82 on the Channel, but its own absolute RMSE against 24 Météo-France coastal SYNOPs is **below 3 m/s on the Channel and Atlantic and below 5 m/s on the Mediterranean** — the same order as the forecast errors one would want to diagnose — and it cycles 3-hourly 3D-Var, so a sea breeze that reverses within an hour is resolved by its forecast fields between cycles, not by its analyses ([Patra et al. 2025](https://asr.copernicus.org/articles/22/69/2025/); [C3S CERRA](https://climate.copernicus.eu/copernicus-regional-reanalysis-europe-cerra)). Two hard prohibitions follow. **ERA5-Land must never be used as a higher-resolution wind reference**: its documentation states the 10 m wind "is only interpolated and not otherwise adjusted from ERA-5", so the apparent gain from 31 km to 9 km is cosmetic ([CDS ERA5-Land](https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-land-timeseries)). And ASCAT offers essentially nothing at SSA's venues: standard products flag wind vector cells within **~70 km (25 km product) or ~35 km (12.5 km product)** of the coast, KNMI's operational coastal product reaches 10–15 km, and KNMI's regression land correction that reaches the coast sees buoy-comparison error rise from **~2.4 m/s beyond 20 km to ~4.2 m/s inside 5 km** ([Remote Sensing 16, 2053](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/remotesensing/remotesensing-16-02053/article_deploy/remotesensing-16-02053.pdf); [KNMI ASCAT coastal](https://scatterometer.knmi.nl/ascat_b_osi_co_prod); [Vogelzang & Stoffelen 2022](https://mdc.coaps.fsu.edu/scatterometry/meeting/docs/2022/IOVWST-2022%20Vogelzang-and-Stoffelen%20ASCAT_land_correction.pdf)). It also measures a stress-equivalent wind rather than an anemometer wind, and its twice-daily sun-synchronous sampling is structurally incapable of catching a sea-breeze onset. For the Solent and the Golfe de Saint-Tropez it is unusable, and no better product rescues it.

## The representativeness floor is 2.8 kn at 9 km, and the law runs out above 20 km

The quantity that limits everything above has a published parameterisation. ECMWF Technical Memorandum 865 models 10 m wind representativeness as a **truncated normal with cut-off at zero**, N₀(μ, σ²), with density (1/σ)·φ((x−μ)/σ)/Φ(μ/σ) for x ≥ 0, where **μ(y_B) = α₀ + α₁·y_B** and **σ(y_B) = β₁·√y_B + ε** with ε = 0.01 to avoid division by zero, and y_B the area-mean wind. The generalised scale law (its Eq. 7) is **α₀(Δ_A) = −0.02·Δ_A, α₁(Δ_A) = 1 + 0.002·Δ_A, β₁(Δ_A) = −0.04·Δ_A + 0.17·Δ_A^0.75**, with Δ_A the averaging scale in km ([Ben Bouallègue 2020, TM865, doi:10.21957/5z6esc7wr](https://www.ecmwf.int/sites/default/files/elibrary/2020/81173-accounting-representativeness-verification-ensemble-forecasts.pdf)). The memo's directly fitted values at ECMWF ENS's ~18 km grid are (α₀ = −0.36, α₁ = 1.036, β₁ = 0.76), which the scale law reproduces — a useful internal check that the arithmetic below is being read correctly. Accounting for representativeness changes ECMWF's own 10 m wind CRPS by "**about 15 % at day 1**, decaying to around 6 % at day 7", and the diagonal elementary skill score "can exceed 10 % for intermediate thresholds (10 or 15 m/s)".

Evaluating Eq. 7 at an area-mean wind of 7.5 m/s (14.6 kn) gives the following, all of it derived arithmetic from the cited formula rather than numbers the memo prints:

| Averaging scale | β₁ | σ at y_B = 7.5 m/s | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 km (SSA's nest) | 0.206 | **1.1 kn** | **Extrapolation far below the fitted range** |
| 9 km (Open-Meteo ICON) | 0.523 | **2.8 kn** | **Extrapolation below the fitted range** |
| 20 km | 0.808 | **4.3 kn** | Smallest fitted scale |
| 40 km | 1.104 | 5.9 kn | Fitted |
| 150 km | 1.287 | 6.9 kn | Largest fitted scale |

Three caveats must travel with every one of those numbers and none of them is cosmetic. **The parameters were estimated only for Δ_A of 20, 30, … 150 km**, so the 2 km and 9 km rows are extrapolations below the fitted range and are not measurements — a point that matters doubly because the function β₁(Δ_A) = −0.04Δ_A + 0.17Δ_A^0.75 has no physical derivation forcing it to behave sensibly as Δ_A → 0, and in fact it goes negative below about 0.07 km. **The fit used the HDOBS high-density network of European land SYNOP stations** (the figure spans roughly 40–60 N, 0–40 E), requiring at least five observations inside each box; nothing in the memo establishes validity over water or across a land–sea boundary, which is exactly where every SSA venue sits and where representativeness error should be largest. And the memo reports that the model **overestimates representativeness error for high wind categories, is positively biased for low and intermediate winds, and underestimates it in autumn and winter while capturing spring well** — so the seasonal direction of the error is against SSA on the shoulder-season days.

Two further consequences fall straight out of the algebra. First, the mean relation μ = α₀ + α₁·y_B crosses μ = y_B at **y_B = 10 m/s for every scale**, since −0.02Δ_A + 0.002Δ_A·y_B = 0 implies y_B = 10 (derived). The model therefore says a point observation sits *below* the grid-box mean in light-to-moderate wind and *above* it in strong wind — the familiar result that a point sees more of the gust and exposure tail than the area average. Second, and this is the number that should discipline every claim SSA makes about its own skill: at 9 km, representativeness σ of **2.8 kn** is the same size as the entire cross-validated RMSE reduction the existing venue MOS claims (2.4 to 3.9 kn cv_rmse against 2.6 to 5.2 kn raw). At ERA5's 31 km the representativeness spread alone is comparable to or larger than the coastal station RMSEs reported in the validation literature, which is the quantitative form of the honest answer: **at global-reanalysis resolution most of the apparent error at a single coastal station is scale mismatch, not model error, and no amount of scoring at that station can separate the two without a denser network.**

The most important thing TM865 says is also the most inconvenient. Its approach is deliberately the **opposite** of what calibration wants: it *widens the forecast* to acknowledge that the point is unknowable from the grid box, rather than *moving the forecast* to the point. For 10 m wind, the representativeness-aware forecast "could be interpreted as valid at any given location of a model grid box"; only for 2 m temperature does the station–model elevation difference make it valid *at the station*. Asked directly whether the correction could be mapped by location, the author's position was that the model incorporates location only through elevation difference and is "a general model which can be used anywhere in the world". **ECMWF's own position is therefore that no general scheme moves a gridded wind forecast to a point.** Any point calibration is implicitly claiming that within-box variability is predictable from exposure, land use and orography rather than random — a claim testable only where several stations sit in or near the same box. SSA's MOS makes exactly this claim, and should say so.

### Can SSA fit its own representativeness law at the Solent? No — but it can measure something better

The honest assessment of a local fit is negative on TM865's own terms and positive on a different target. TM865's method needs **at least five observations inside each box**, and a *scale law* needs boxes at many scales. At 20 km the Solent gives one or two boxes; at the 2–9 km scales SSA actually runs, a box holds perhaps two to six stations of which zero to two are professional, and the rest are garden anemometers whose mutual variance is dominated by sheltering rather than by atmospheric variability. Fitting σ(Δ_A) from that would produce a curve measuring exposure heterogeneity and calling it representativeness — the worst kind of wrong answer, because it would look like a measurement.

What **is** feasible, and is worth doing, is the quantity representativeness error is built out of: a **pairwise structure function over water**. For every pair of trusted Solent stations, compute the variance of the simultaneous speed difference as a function of separation, stratified by onshore versus offshore flow and by speed band, using only sea-surface sites. With Bramble Bank, Nab Tower, the ABP masts, the two CCO piers and the three WeatherFile sites when their token lands, that is roughly nine stations and **about 36 pairs spanning separations of 1 to 30 km** — enough to estimate a semivariogram of the over-water 10 m wind field at exactly the scales TM865 does not cover. What it would establish: the local decorrelation structure over water, hence an empirical floor on how well any point forecast can possibly do, and a direct test of whether the Solent's over-water variability is larger or smaller than the extrapolated land-based law predicts. What it would **not** establish: a box-mean representativeness distribution (that needs five-plus stations inside a common box, which the Solent does not provide), nor anything about land–sea asymmetry without the onshore/offshore stratification, nor a transferable law for Porto Cervo or Sorrento, where the professional station count is far lower. Barbara Casati's framing is the right standard for reading the result: if point observations and gridded observations give similar answers, good — "otherwise, have a problem" ([2020 IVMW-O session notes](https://webdata.img.univie.ac.at/jwgvfr/Representativeness/Notes_2020IVMWO_Representiveness&ObsUncertainty_10Nov15UTC.pdf)), and in the same discussion she noted that "sometimes differences in observations are greater than differences between observations and models".

## Reverse the height correction: push the model down, and let one coefficient absorb the height

The current design refuses to correct to 10 m when height is unknown (`to10m` returns null in `/Users/wouterverbraak/Code/ssa/src/lib/windObs.ts`) and lifts the observation to 10 m when it can. The refusal is the right instinct and the wrong endpoint, and the lifting is the wrong direction. The reasoning runs through three quantitative facts.

**Roughness barely matters over water; the land/sea decision is the whole of it.** Evaluating the neutral log law over the Davenport–Wieringa classes (derived arithmetic; class values from [wind-stats](https://wind-stats.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_guide/roughness_length.html), provenance [Wieringa 1992](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/016761059290434C)) gives a 10→30 m ratio of **1.10 over open sea (z₀ = 2×10⁻⁴ m), 1.12 over coastal water, 1.19 over open grassland, 1.48 over suburb or forest**. The spread across all *water* classes is 2.5 % at 30 m; marine to grassland is 8 %; marine to suburban is 34 %. Closing the marine problem self-consistently with COARE 3.5 (Charnock coefficient linear in 10 m neutral wind, a₁ = 0.0017 s/m, a₂ = −0.0050, capped at 19 m/s, from [coare35vn.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NOAA-PSL/COARE-algorithm/master/Python/COARE3.5/coare35vn.py), introduced by [Edson et al. 2013](https://core.ac.uk/works/64777322)) gives a neutral 10→30 m ratio of **1.084 at 5 kn rising only to 1.119 at 30 kn — the entire wind-speed dependence of marine roughness is worth 3.2 % at masthead height** (derived), with a shallow minimum near 8 kn because the aerodynamically smooth term raises z₀ again as u\* falls. COARE 3.0 and 3.5 disagree on z₀ by up to a factor of two at 8–10 kn and agree on the *ratio* to 0.4 %, which is a useful robustness result: the profile ratio is far better conditioned than z₀ itself. Also, over the narrow 10–35 m band the power law is an acceptable computational shortcut *if* α comes from z₀ (under 1 % error), and a bad one if α comes from a table: **1/7 = 0.143 over-predicts the 30 m marine wind by 6.2 %, the offshore-wind convention α ≈ 0.11 by 2.4 %, and the right marine near-neutral value for a 10→35 m problem is α ≈ 0.07–0.09** (derived) — consistent with the measured offshore finding that **more than 80 % of observed shear exponents are below 0.14, with negative exponents more common than over land** ([CityU lidar study](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/characterising-wind-shear-exponents-in-the-offshore-area-using-li/)).

**Stability is the dominant term, it is asymmetric, and it bites hardest in light air.** Closing the bulk problem with COARE 3.5 roughness and the Holtslag–De Bruin / Beljaars–Holtslag stable ψ_m (a = 1, b = 0.667, c = 5, d = 0.35) and the Paulson unstable integral, parameterised by air–sea temperature difference, gives the following derived numbers for the classic warm-land-air-over-cooler-water case that defines a Mediterranean or Solent morning. At **10 kn and ΔT = +2 K** — an entirely ordinary offshore-breeze morning — the 10→30 m increase is **+29.7 % instead of +8.4 %**: a neutral log law returns 10.84 kn at masthead when the truth is **12.97 kn, a 2.1 kn (16 %) underestimate**. At 6 kn and +2 K the increase is +30.4 %; at 20 kn and +2 K it is only +4.4 % (0.97 kn), because mechanical mixing wins. The unstable branch is nearly flat and nearly harmless, **−2.7 % to −5 %, i.e. 0.3–0.7 kn across all realistic negative ΔT**. If one branch must be ignored, ignore the unstable one. Two validity flags attach. Beyond ζ ≈ 0.2 the two stable ψ functions diverge badly — at z/L = 0.5 the Beljaars–Holtslag ratio is 1.305 against 1.386 for the linear ψ_m = −5ζ, a **6 % disagreement at 30 m from the choice of function alone, rising to 16 % at ζ = 1.0** (derived) — and the strongly stable rows sit beyond the 0 < ζ < 1 range Businger et al. state and into the regime where SHEBA finds turbulence collapsing at a critical Richardson number of about 0.20–0.25. In that regime the defensible answer is a **bound, not a number**: the masthead wind can be 25–40 % above the 10 m wind and no surface-layer theory will pin it down better. Worse, a stable marine boundary layer of 50–150 m puts the constant-flux surface layer at 5–15 m, so **30 m is outside it** exactly when the correction is largest. And because NWP PBL schemes "forecast more moderated stable stratifications" than FINO1 observes ([DTU Orbit](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/e7e9cb82-8452-41fb-8580-7c3fe801b52c)), a model-supplied L biases the correction *toward neutral* — under-correcting the case that matters. At Östergarnsholm the stability distribution is **unstable 18 %, weakly unstable 30 %, neutral 15 %, weakly stable 25 %, stable 12 %** ([Hallgren et al. 2022](https://wes.copernicus.org/articles/7/1183/2022/)), so a neutral-only correction is applied in the wrong regime roughly 85 % of the time and specifically under-corrects on the ~37 % of occasions that are the light-air days.

**The race course is inside the marine internal boundary layer; the pier station is not.** Measured IBL heights of **60–150 m for land fetches of 400–2500 m** at a Black Sea coastal site ([Raabe & Foken](https://www.bayceer.uni-bayreuth.de/mm/de/pub/html/18380Raabe_Foken.pdf)), with the conventional √fetch scaling, put a 30 m IBL depth at a fetch of order **100–300 m** in neutral or unstable flow (derived). A course 1–2 nm offshore is comfortably inside the marine IBL at masthead height, and the equilibrium-fetch literature — tens of kilometres convective, several hundred kilometres stable, after Garratt 1987 via [Rowe et al. 2011](https://downloads.psl.noaa.gov/BLO/Air-Sea/southern_ocean/pubs/rowe_BOUN-S-10-00094-2_2011.pdf) — is about the *whole* boundary layer and should not be used to argue otherwise. A pier station with a hundred metres of water fetch, by the same scaling, has a marine IBL only ~15–30 m deep: a 10 m anemometer is inside it, a 30 m extrapolation reaches its top. KNMI's finding that the Rijkswaterstaat blending-height assumption — that the wind at 60 m is independent of local roughness — **fails at land–water transitions** ([KNMI TR-381](https://cdn.knmi.nl/system/data_center_publications/files/000/071/571/original/TR381.pdf)) is the formal statement of why pier and headland stations are the hard case: the standard exposure machinery is least reliable exactly where sailing stations live.

### The design change, with its reasoning

Verify at the station's own level and transfer the **model** down to it. Four reasons, in order of force. The model knows its own stability and roughness, so a downward transfer is internally consistent, whereas lifting the observation requires SSA to supply an L and a z₀ it does not have. Assuming 10 m is a **bias, not a random error**, and it will be silently absorbed into any coefficient fitted on top of it — the derived bias from assuming 10 m when the true height is h is **−4.7 % at 6 m and +3.7 % at 15 m over open sea, but −8.8 % and +7.0 % over grass and −22 % to +18 % in a suburb**, so for a garden or club station the error is large and station-specific. The height is **weakly identified** and should never be reported as a number: over open sea at h ≈ 10 m, one metre of height is worth only **0.93 % in the ratio**, so pinning the height to ±1 m requires the regression slope known to better than 1 %, far beyond what cup-anemometer calibration, sheltering and the spatial decorrelation of wind between two stations allow (derived). And a station below the displacement height of its surroundings has no log-law answer at all: for a 15–20 m canopy the conventional d ≈ ⅔h is 10–13 m, so a 10 m anemometer sits at or below d and ln((z−d)/z₀) is undefined — which is the one case where `to10m` returning null is not merely cautious but correct in principle.

So: **fit a single multiplicative coefficient per wind-direction sector**, k_sector, absorbing height, exposure, sheltering and sensor calibration together, bounded to a physically plausible interval (over water k ∈ [0.89, 1.09] for h ∈ [3, 25] m; over grass k ∈ [0.79, 1.16]), conditioned on stability class or at least on season and time of day as a proxy, because the derived tables show the required coefficient varies by 20–30 % between stable and neutral and a stability-blind coefficient will fit the near-neutral mode and be wrong by 10–30 % on the stable mornings. This is KNMI's exposure-correction-factor method generalised: KNMI determines ECFs **from the wind measurements themselves, per 20° wind-direction sector**, from gust-factor or σ_u analysis, reflecting roughness out to about 2 km from the site, with the sigma method better but requiring 10-minute data ([KNMI potential wind](https://www.knmi.nl/research/observations-data-technology/projects/potential-wind)). Verkaik's published error bars for the whole procedure are **up to 10 % between two gustiness models over rough terrain and under 5 % from the neutral-stratification assumption and the blending-height choice** ([Verkaik 2000](https://www.knmi.nl/research/publications/evaluation-of-two-gustiness-models-for-exposure-correction-calculations)), with the sigma method giving more consistent corrections ([KNMI WR 2009-02](https://cdn.knmi.nl/knmi/pdf/bibliotheek/knmipubWR/WR2009-02.pdf)). Two important qualifications: Verkaik's "<5 % from the neutral assumption" is for a 10 m potential wind over land, and must **not** be carried into a 10→30 m marine extrapolation where the derived stability term is 10–30 %; and the WMO procedure deliberately targets potential wind at 10 m over z₀ = 0.03 m, a standardised *land* wind, so chaining it to a masthead product without a second transfer from 10 m/0.03 m to 30 m over marine z₀ is a ~9 % error (derived from the class ratios above). **SSA already stores `gust_kn` alongside `tws_kn` in `wind_observations`**, which means the gust-factor route to a per-sector effective roughness is available today for every station that reports a gust — this is the single most promising unexploited asset in the schema, and the only published method for inferring exposure without metadata. No published method exists for inferring an anemometer height from wind data alone; multiple searches found nothing, and the per-sector-coefficient recommendation is this report's synthesis of the KNMI precedent plus the derived identifiability analysis, not a citable standard.

The uncertainty this adds should be stated, not hidden. Treating the correction as multiplicative and log-normal (U_mast = k_h · k_z0 · k_stab · U_obs), with per-term 1σ values of ~3 % for an unknown height assumed at 10 m over water, ~1.5 % for marine z₀, ~6 % if the sector is ambiguous land/sea, and 3–6 % for stability corrected with a modelled L against ~8 % with a −10 to −30 % tail if stability is ignored, gives a total of **4.5–7 % for a corrected over-water extrapolation and ~9 %, skewed low, if stability is ignored** (derived, constructed from the individual cited and derived magnitudes; no literature was found on uncertainty propagation for height extrapolation into verification scores). If the model's own error is 10–15 %, the extrapolation is a third to a half of the budget in quadrature. A masthead verification statistic that does not state this is overstating its own precision. The clean architecture follows: keep the extrapolation **out of the verification loop** entirely, verify at station level, and produce the masthead wind as a separate, clearly labelled product step carrying its own error bar.

## The MOS that ships measures a boat; an observation refit measures a venue, and they are not the same quantity

Here is what the state of the art says, and then what SSA's own coefficients say when read against it.

**The parametric family dominates and its ceiling is stable.** For wind speed, non-homogeneous regression / EMOS with a distribution respecting the zero bound — truncated normal, log-normal, truncated GEV, or a regime-switching mixture — buys roughly **8–18 % CRPS over a raw ensemble** across every ensemble, country and decade examined. Lerch & Thorarinsdottir on 228 German stations and 83,220 ECMWF cases: day-1 CRPS of **climatology 1.54, raw ensemble 1.26, truncated normal 1.05, GEV 1.04, combination 1.03**, i.e. 16.7 %, 17.5 % and 18.3 % over raw (derived from their table, [arXiv:1305.2026](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1305.2026)); Baran & Lerch's regime-switching TN-LN reached 18.2–18.3 % on two large sets but only **8.2 % on a 10-city, 313-day Hungarian set** — roughly half, on the small sample ([arXiv:1407.3252](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1407.3252)). Machine learning adds a further 5–20 % relative but only with large training sets: Schulz & Lerch's gust comparison on 175 German stations gives **EMOS 0.95, EMOS-GB 0.88, QRF 0.87, DRN 0.84** against raw 1.33, i.e. basic methods 26–29 % and neural networks 35–37 % ([arXiv:2106.09512](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.09512)). The choice of distribution is worth **≤1–2 % CRPS in the body and 4.5–9.5 % in the upper tail**, so it matters for a race-cancellation decision far more than for a mean-wind forecast. **QRF is specifically the wrong tool on a short record**: it gained the most of any method from more data (CRPS 0.95 → 0.81 between 180,849 and 1,626,724 samples) and its predicted quantiles "are restricted to the range of observed values in the training period" ([arXiv:1805.09091](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.09091)).

**Training data: the rolling window is short, the local fit is not.** The operational consensus for rolling-window EMOS on wind speed is **25–90 days**, most studies landing at 30–60; **25 days is the calibration floor** (below it the predictive intervals stop being calibrated — 90 % coverage stabilises only above 24 days, [arXiv:1202.4442](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1202.4442)), and scores change by under 1 % across windows of 20–50 days. But *local* (per-station) estimation is an order of magnitude hungrier: Lerch & Baran cite **365–1816 days** as optimal for local fitting, and on their own 161-day dataset a 15-parameter local EMOS "could not be estimated at all" ([arXiv:1509.03521](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1509.03521)). That paper is also the closest published precedent to SSA's situation and the highest-leverage move available: **semi-local pooling by similarity, with L = 3 similar stations, reached CRPS 0.766 against regional 0.955 and raw 1.058 — about 20 % better than regional and 3 % better than local — on a dataset too short for local fitting at all**, and the best similarity metric was **climatology plus forecast-error distribution, not geographic distance**. Adaptive re-clustering each training period gained a further 1–5 %. The neural-network equivalent is a station embedding of length 2, worth about 10 % over a global model.

**Stratify by lead time always, by season with over a year of data, by regime only with a gating test, by direction sector only where each sector has the observations.** Lead-time stratification is universal and undebated. Schulz & Lerch's seasonal scheme — twelve monthly parameter sets per station, each drawing on the previous, current and next month — beat both rolling windows and training on the full set. Regime conditioning works but not always, and the Met Office NAO work is the clearest published statement of the trade-off: the mixture model "offers substantial improvements … when the local wind speed depends strongly on the NAO" but "the additional complexity of the model can hinder forecast performance otherwise", which is why they apply it only when a regime-dependency measure exceeds a threshold ([Exeter 130334](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/130334?show=full)). Direction-sector stratification is specifically flagged as unreliable: the Schuhen approach "can be unreliable at stations with few observations per sector" ([ASCMO 5, 115](https://ascmo.copernicus.org/articles/5/115/2019/)). For a coastal venue the meteorologically obvious stratifications — sea breeze on/off, offshore versus onshore, stability — are exactly the ones that split the sample fastest, and the defensible move from this literature is to put them in as **predictors** (day of year, u/v, land–sea temperature difference, shortwave radiation) in one pooled model rather than as hard bins. No paper was found that stratifies wind post-processing by explicit stability class; the signal appears only through boundary-layer proxies in neural-network feature importance, which is a genuine gap in the sourced literature and an opening for SSA, which has air and sea temperature available.

**Direction needs its own machinery and its own score.** Two incompatible routes exist: circular statistics (von Mises plus circular–circular regression, [Bao et al. 2010](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Bao2010MWR.pdf); circular regression forests, [arXiv:2001.00412](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.00412)) and u/v vector decomposition (bivariate normal with direction-dependent correlation, [Schuhen et al. 2012](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1201.2612)). Route (b) dominates recent work and gives the better numbers — local bivariate EMOS cut bMAE by 13 % and the energy score by 24 % against the raw ensemble — but **it does not evaluate direction calibration at all**: every paper verifies the vector, not the angle, and no paper was found reporting a percentage skill improvement for wind *direction* from a u/v method. For a sailing product where direction and its shifts *are* the deliverable, that silence is disqualifying on its own: an energy score of 1.08 says nothing about whether a 10° shift forecast is reliable. The defensible plan is to **calibrate u/v jointly and verify with a circular CRPS on the implied angle**. Two sobering data points on expectations. Lang et al.'s circular forests gained **13–25 % at Innsbruck and 58–71 % at Vienna**, and the explanation is directly transferable: Innsbruck's gains were smaller "because its few dominant wind regimes make climatology comparatively strong". A coastal venue with a dominant sea-breeze direction is the Innsbruck case — climatology is a hard baseline and the post-processing margin is small. And the 2026 bivariate comparison on 60 German stations found the direction-dependent correlation term moved the energy score from **1.1862 to 1.1858 — nothing** — where Schuhen et al. found 18 % on an 8-member ensemble ([arXiv:2601.21401](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21401v1)), suggesting the term was substituting for ensemble under-dispersion rather than capturing physics. Do not import the Schuhen parameterisation uncritically.

**Score with CRPS, not RMSE, or the project will conclude post-processing does not work.** On the same Hungarian data, BMA against the raw ensemble improved **CRPS by 11.9 %, MAE by 4.8 % and RMSE by only 2.6 %** ([arXiv:1202.4442](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1202.4442)); Baran & Baran report CRPSS of 11.1 % while "median MAE differences from raw ensemble are under 0.1 m/s at all lead times" ([arXiv:2104.14910](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.14910)). The gain is in the shape of the distribution, and only a proper probabilistic score sees it. Add threshold-weighted CRPS at the operationally meaningful thresholds (for sailing, the race-limit wind), PIT or rank histograms for calibration, and an explicit skill score against local climatology — which is a demanding benchmark, since climatology beat the raw ensemble by only 6–18 % CRPS in these studies, and a post-processor that cannot beat local climatology is doing nothing. Keep calibration and accuracy as separate diagnostics: the Hungarian study's coverage went from 38.7 %/55.1 % to 68.1 %/90.3 % against nominal 66.7 %/90 %, but **bought with a near-doubling of interval width**, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test still rejected uniformity (p = 0.022, 0.019). On validation: use **blocked, time-based splits**, never random k-fold. The wind literature uses held-out years or leave-one-year-out almost universally, and Veldkamp et al.'s design — three folds with **about six months separating training, validation and test**, plus block bootstrapping over 1000 resamples to account for spatial correlation between stations — is the most conservative and the one to copy when a whole held-out year is unaffordable ([arXiv:2007.04005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.04005)). The magnitude of the leakage being avoided is quantified only in adjacent fields — random splits inflating error estimates by **up to 40 %** and AUC by **as much as 0.16** ([arXiv:2005.14263](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.14263); [arXiv:2502.03480](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03480v2)) — so treat those as orders of magnitude, not wind-specific numbers. For SSA the leakage risk is doubly structural: nearby venues share synoptic systems, so any pooled fit must block by time for all venues simultaneously. Finally, on regularisation, Rasp & Lerch's candid report is worth following exactly: **early stopping and averaging over an ensemble of ten fits worked; dropout and L2 weight decay "were not successful in our case"; extra hidden layers, input-noise augmentation, and convolutional or recurrent architectures overfit without improving validation.** The effective regulariser in this field is variance reduction by averaging, not shrinkage.

### Reading SSA's own coefficients

The shipped fits in `src/components/weather/mos/` are deterministic conditional-mean corrections of the form corrected = a·raw + b, applied to a 30 m wind, with optional diurnal and sector terms, fitted on n = 131–445 paired cases per model per venue:

| Venue | slope a | intercept b (kn) | n | cv_rmse → raw_rmse (kn) | reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| St Tropez | 0.805 – 0.972 | +1.38 to +3.26 | 267–417 | 2.36–3.04 ← 2.56–3.43 | 4.9–18.6 % |
| Porto Cervo | 0.856 – 1.063 | +0.81 to +2.59 | 131–261 | 2.63–3.83 ← 3.01–3.91 | 2.0–18.5 % |
| Sorrento | **0.521 – 0.735** | **+4.96 to +6.25** | 287–445 | 3.46–3.74 ← 4.16–5.17 | 9.9–29.0 % |

Four readings, all this report's own, follow from those numbers. First, the sample sizes are not the problem: at n = 261 with a six-parameter sector model that is ~43 cases per parameter, above the 10–20 per parameter rule of thumb the literature implies. **What the fits are fitted *to* is the problem, not how many.** Second, the fitted intercepts at St Tropez and Porto Cervo (mean +2.4 and +1.8 kn) are **the same size as the derived stable-case deficit of a neutral log-law masthead extrapolation — 1.26 to 3.19 kn at 10 kn for ΔT of +1 to +5 K**. That is not a coincidence to be assumed, but it is a hypothesis with a sharp test: if a large share of b is a missing stability term rather than model bias, then adding a stability-aware downward transfer should shrink b substantially, and an observation-based refit that already corrects for stability will produce a much smaller intercept. Third, **Sorrento's a ≈ 0.52 with b ≈ +6.25 kn is a regression-to-the-mean artefact and should not ship as a forecast.** It maps a raw range of 5–25 kn onto 8.9–19.3 kn: the correction is optimal for squared error and compresses everything toward about 13 kn, so the corrected forecast can never say 25 kn however hard the model blows. That is precisely the failure the EMOS literature exists to avoid — a conditional mean is not a forecast distribution, and the right fix is to model the spread as a second equation (the truncated-normal b₀ + b₁S² form) rather than to flatten the mean. Fourth, the per-sector delta is necessarily keyed on the *model's* sector, and the stored `sector_agreement` of **0.70–0.83** says the model places the wind in the wrong sector 17–30 % of the time, so the sector term is being applied to the wrong bucket in up to three cases in ten — which dilutes its benefit by roughly that fraction and is a concrete reason the sector models do not beat the plain bias-scale ones by more.

The deeper issue is the one the file header states itself: the coefficients were fitted against **three years of one boat's Northstar masthead anemometer**. That measurement is not a reference-grade wind. It carries the instrument's own calibration error, masthead upwash and flow distortion from the sail plan, and the boat's motion, and because it is a single platform there is no second sensor anywhere in the fit to separate those from the model's bias. The quantity being corrected is therefore "what that boat's masthead reads at that venue", and the published analogue of the problem is the Met Office's warning about its own bias tool: it "considers a total bias from all the sources of difference … It does not attempt to attribute the differences to known causes" ([Met Office EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_MetOffice_Final_2022.pdf)). An observation-based refit against Port-Log masts, RADOME stations and buoys measures a **different quantity**: the 10 m wind at a fixed, surveyed, open-exposure site. It will not reproduce the current intercepts and should not be expected to; if it did, that would be evidence the masthead was accidentally a good 10 m anemometer. The honest consequence is that the two cannot be blended or validated against each other. The right architecture keeps them separate: **fit and verify the model correction against observations at station level**, and treat the step from a corrected 10 m venue wind to a 30 m masthead wind as a *second, separately calibrated and separately error-barred* transfer, which is where the boat's own log legitimately belongs — because the boat's masthead is the only sensor that ever measures that quantity. Note also what three years of one boat's log is not: continuous coverage. The n of 131–445 implies on the order of a few hundred race-day hours per venue, which is why the existing fits cannot be stratified further without collapsing, and why the pooling-by-similarity result above matters so much.

| 5 | **Fit one per-sector multiplicative coefficient per station, conditioned on stability class, bounded to the physical interval; never report an estimated height.** Derive effective roughness per sector from the stored `gust_kn` where available. | KNMI's ECF method per 20° sector, inferred from the station's own turbulence statistics, with published error bars of ≤10 % and ≤5 % ([KNMI potential wind](https://www.knmi.nl/research/observations-data-technology/projects/potential-wind); [Verkaik 2000](https://www.knmi.nl/research/publications/evaluation-of-two-gustiness-models-for-exposure-correction-calculations)). Exposure error is direction-dependent, not a scalar. Height is weakly identified at 0.93 %/m (derived) and will absorb every other multiplicative error. `gust_kn` is already in the schema and unused for this. |
| 6 | **Build the QC battery in the published order, with the persistence test split into zero and non-zero arms**, plus a gust-below-mean check, and a monthly O−B monitoring blacklist on the ECMWF pattern with the Met Office's "3 rejects in a month" review cadence. | Range faults are cheap and real (>1,500 km/h; speed reported as a string). The zero/non-zero split is the only documented fix for the calm-versus-dead collision ([KNMI EUMETNET 2022](https://www.eumetnet.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Crowdsourcing-PWS-Quality-Control-A1.05_KNMI_Final_2022.pdf)). Siting faults are invisible to every internal test and visible only in O−B. Calibrate streak thresholds on the fit tier, because no published threshold exists. |
| 7 | **Replace the deterministic a·raw + b with a truncated-normal EMOS** (a₀, a₁ for the mean; b₀, b₁ for the spread), on a 25–30-day rolling window, **pooled across the nine venues by similarity of climatology and forecast-error distribution** rather than by distance, with per-venue intercepts only. Score with CRPS, twCRPS at the race-limit wind, PIT histograms, and a skill score against local climatology. | 4 parameters is the smallest model that is both calibrated and fittable at SSA's N ([arXiv:1202.4442](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1202.4442); [arXiv:1509.03521](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1509.03521)). Pooling with L=3 similar stations gave 20 % over regional on a 161-day record. Expect ~10 % CRPS and treat >20 % as evidence of a leaky validation. A conditional mean with a=0.52 cannot forecast a windy day. |
| 8 | **Verify with HiRA/SO-NF at each fit-tier station plus explicit sea-breeze onset timing in minutes.** Use Geography Match or nearest-sea-grid-point interpolation, never bilinear across a coastline, and never MET's *Best* option. | Four of five spatial families need a gridded truth SSA will never have. HiRA credits the model for being right nearby under a proper score; *Best* uses the observation to pick the forecast and is unusable for calibration ([MET Point-Stat](https://metplus.readthedocs.io/projects/met/en/latest/Users_Guide/point-stat.html)). Report displacement as minutes and knots, because an RMSE at fixed times prefers a model that produced no front (2.5× in the derived worked example). |
| 9 | **Verify the 1–2 km nest against sea stations only, on timing and structure.** Expect it to lose on RMSE where it wins on physics. | MET Norway's 10 km → 1 km move made coastal errors *larger* through land/sea tiling ([IVMW-O notes](https://webdata.img.univie.ac.at/jwgvfr/Representativeness/Notes_2020IVMWO_Representiveness&ObsUncertainty_10Nov15UTC.pdf)); the model land–sea transition spans tens of kilometres. The nest is the only thing SSA runs that can resolve a front at all. |
| 10 | **Measure the Solent over-water structure function** — simultaneous speed-difference variance against separation for all pairs of trusted sea stations, stratified by onshore/offshore and speed band. | ~9 stations give ~36 pairs over 1–30 km at exactly the scales TM865 does not cover. Establishes the local decorrelation floor over water. Does **not** establish a box-mean representativeness distribution (needs ≥5 stations per box) and does not transfer to Porto Cervo or Sorrento. |
| 11 | **Publish a representativeness floor alongside every score**, from TM865's Eq. 7 at the model's own grid scale, labelled as extrapolation below 20 km. | At 9 km the derived σ is **2.8 kn at an area-mean 14.6 kn** — comparable to the entire RMSE reduction the current MOS claims. Accounting for representativeness changed ECMWF's own day-1 10 m wind CRPS by ~15 %. Stating the floor is the difference between a verification result and a number. |
| 12 | **Hold the boat-log MOS as the masthead transfer, not as the model correction**, and refit it separately once an observation-based 10 m correction exists. | The two fits measure different quantities. The boat's masthead is the only sensor that measures masthead wind, so its log belongs in the transfer step, where its instrument error is a property of the thing being predicted rather than a contaminant in the predictor. |

Items deliberately **not** recommended: QRF or any neural post-processor (wrong tool at this N, and QRF's quantiles cannot exceed the training range); ERA5-Land as a wind reference (its 10 m wind is interpolated ERA5); ASCAT at any SSA venue (flagged out to 35–70 km, 10–15 km at best, error nearly doubling inside 5 km, stress-equivalent, twice daily); hard direction-sector stratification at venues with thin per-sector samples (put direction in as a predictor instead); and any 2-D spatial score computed from the garden-station network.

## Conclusion

The most useful shift in understanding here is that two of SSA's apparently separate problems are the same problem wearing different clothes. The unknown-anemometer-height question and the unrepresentative-station question both resolve to a **single multiplicative coefficient per direction sector**, fitted from data and bounded by physics, because height, exposure, sheltering and sensor calibration are not separately identifiable from wind data alone — one metre of height is worth 0.93 % in the profile ratio, which is inside cup-anemometer calibration error. Chasing the height is chasing a parameter that will silently absorb everything else; fitting the sector coefficient and refusing to name a height is strictly more honest and strictly more useful. The same collapse happens on the verification side: the double penalty and representativeness error are both artefacts of comparing a point to an area, and they push in opposite directions — the first makes a sharp forecast look worse than it is, the second makes an ensemble look under-dispersed. Fixing one without naming the other produces a number that cannot be interpreted.

The second shift is about what the existing MOS has actually been doing. Its intercepts are the right size to be a missing stability term as much as a model bias, which means the current pipeline has been paying for a physics error with a statistical patch — and the patch worked, in the sense that cv_rmse fell, which is exactly why nobody noticed. That is the characteristic failure mode of a conditional-mean correction: it absorbs anything systematic, labels all of it "bias", and ships a forecast that has been quietly flattened toward the venue mean (Sorrento's a = 0.52 can never say 25 kn). Separating the model correction from the height transfer, and verifying each against the quantity it actually predicts, is what turns a working patch into something that can be improved. The sharp near-term test is available without new data: add a stability-aware downward transfer and watch whether the fitted intercepts shrink. If they do, most of what has been called model bias at these venues was never the model's fault. Everything else on the list can wait a quarter; the retention change cannot, because the observations SSA is not keeping this month are the training set it will want in 2029.
